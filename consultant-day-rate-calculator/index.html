Why the "Rule of 100" Dictates Consultant Solvency

First-time consultants often make the mistake of multiplying 250 working days by their corporate day rate equivalent, assuming high continuous utilization. In reality, successful independent consultants deliver client work only 100 to 120 days per year:

  • Business Development & Pipeline Lead Time: Generating high-ticket consulting contracts requires consultative sales discovery, multi-stakeholder presentations, and tailored proposals—consuming 30% to 40% of non-billable time.
  • Bench Time & Project Seasonality: Corporate budgets pause during Q4 planning cycles and summer leadership holidays. Your active delivery rate must comfortably subsidize 2 to 3 months of annual bench time.
  • Intellectual Capital Investment: Remaining competitive demands ongoing market research, proprietary framework development, and industry speaking engagements.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What is the standard formula to calculate a consultant day rate from a salary?

The classic consulting rule-of-thumb is: Target Annual Base Salary divided by 100 to 120 billable days. For example, a $150,000 corporate equivalent salary translates to a day rate of $1,250 to $1,500 per day. This accounts for unbillable business development, corporate overhead, taxes, and bench time.

Why do consultants bill by the day rather than by the hour?

Consulting work involves strategic cognitive context, executive stakeholder alignment, and unscheduled advisory problem-solving. Hourly billing commoditizes strategic advice and encourages micromanagement, whereas day rates and weekly advisory sprints anchor pricing to business outcomes.

How many days per year does an independent consultant actually bill?

A full-time independent consultant realistically bills between 100 and 130 days annually (around 10 to 12 days per month). The remaining 100+ business days are required for sales pipeline development, thought leadership, client pitches, administrative bookkeeping, and rest.

What expenses must an independent consultant deduct from gross revenue?

Key business deductions include Errors & Omissions (E&O) professional liability insurance, legal entity maintenance, CPA and bookkeeping services, client travel and lodging, research database subscriptions (Gartner, Statista), and home office overhead.

How do advisory retainers work for senior consultants?

An advisory retainer provides ongoing executive access (e.g., bi-weekly strategic steering calls, asynchronous Slack/email feedback, document reviews). Typical retainers range from $3,500 to $10,000+ per month per client, offering predictable revenue without requiring continuous on-site presence.
